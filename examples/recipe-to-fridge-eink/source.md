## The artifact (author's original prose)

There is a version of this dish in every kitchen around the Mediterranean,
and the only thing they agree on is that it should not be rushed at the
start and should not be fussed with at the end. Mine leans on preserved
lemon — the kind that's been sitting in salt since last winter — because a
preserved lemon does something fresh lemon can't: it goes savory. If you
don't have one, a quick cheat is to simmer strips of fresh lemon peel in
heavily salted water for ten minutes, though the real thing is worth the
jar. Count on about an hour and a quarter from the moment you pick up the
salt box, and it serves four, generously.

Start well before you're hungry: salt eight bone-in, skin-on chicken thighs
on every side with about a tablespoon of kosher salt, and let them sit at
least thirty minutes at room temperature — though if you can do this in the
morning, or even the night before, uncovered in the fridge, the skin will
dry out and crisp far better and the seasoning will reach the bone. This
waiting is the only hard part of the recipe.

Set the thighs skin side down in a wide, dry, cold ovenproof pan — no oil,
the thighs bring their own — and only then turn the heat to medium. Render
slowly, and leave them entirely alone: resist every urge to peek or shuffle
them for a good seven or eight minutes, until the skin releases from the pan
by itself and has gone the color of dark caramel. Flip them for just a
couple of minutes to seal the other side, then set them on a plate. Pour off
all but about two tablespoons of the fat (save the rest — it's liquid gold
for roast potatoes).

In that fat, soften a yellow onion sliced into thin half-moons, about five
minutes, until it slumps but before it browns. Add four sliced garlic
cloves, a teaspoon and a half of ground coriander, and a teaspoon of sweet
paprika and stir for a minute, until your kitchen tells you it's working.
Now pour in a cup and a half of chicken stock, scraping up the browned crust
on the pan floor — that crust is the sauce. Tuck in the slivered rind of one
preserved lemon (flesh scraped away and discarded — it's all salt) and a cup
of pitted green olives, Castelvetranos if you can find them, lightly smashed.

Nestle the thighs back in skin side up, and here is the rule the whole dish
hangs on: the skin must sit proud of the liquid. You braise the meat and
roast the skin at the same time, and if the skin goes under, it turns to a
sad gray raincoat. Slide the pan, uncovered, onto the middle rack of a 425°F
oven for forty to forty-five minutes, until the thighs are cooked through,
the braise has tightened, and the skin is the deep mahogany of old furniture.

Rest everything ten minutes in the pan — the meat relaxes and the sauce
settles. Shower with a handful of roughly chopped parsley and bring the whole
pan to the table, with a lot of crusty bread; the pan juices are not
optional. A pinch of saffron in the stock is lovely if you keep saffron
around, and leftovers, if they exist, are even better shredded into rice the
next day.

## Intent

Get a specific dinner cooked correctly on a weeknight. The two facts that
ruin the dish if missed: oven temperature (it's a hot, uncovered
braise-roast — unusual) and the skin-above-the-liquid rule. The prose voice
exists for *reading pleasure*; mid-cooking it's an obstacle.

## Audience & use context

The author, cooking. Eyes at 0.5–1.5 m from the fridge door, hands wet or
holding a pan, kitchen light, glances of 1–3 seconds. They will look up
*"what number step am I on and what's next"* roughly eight times, and *"what
temp / how long"* from across the kitchen at least twice.

## Design language

The author's recipe notes have none. The kitchen does: the fridge panel
hangs next to handwritten magnetic lists — high contrast, utilitarian, no
decoration survives steam and toddler magnets. Chosen: Archivo family
(strong grotesque, big counters at 138 PPI), pure black/white.

## Voice

Warm but bossy — "leave them entirely alone", "the pan juices are not
optional". Condensed steps should keep the bossiness ("Don't move them.")
and shed the storytelling.
